# Hiett v. Lake Barcroft Community Ass'n, 244 Va. 191, 418 S.E.2d 894 (1992)

Fetched via Wayback copy of Justia (`web.archive.org/web/2024id_/https://law.justia.com/cases/virginia/supreme-court/1992/911395-1.html`); live Justia and CourtListener were bot-blocked. Citations confirmed from CourtListener search API: 244 Va. 191; 418 S.E.2d 894; 8 Va. Law Rep. 3381; Record No. 911395; decided 1992-06-05; cited by 30 opinions in CourtListener.

## Question presented

"The primary issue in this appeal is whether a pre-injury release from liability for negligence is void as being against public policy."

## Facts

- Plaintiff Robert D. Hiett became quadriplegic at the start of the swim leg of the "Teflon Man Triathlon," sponsored by the Lake Barcroft Community Association (LABARCA). He waded in to thigh depth, dove, and hit his head on the lake bottom or an object under the water.
- All participants had to sign an entry form. Its first sentence: "...I hereby, for myself, my heirs, and executors waive, release and forever discharge any and all rights and claims for damages which I may have or m[a]y hereafter accrue to me against the organizers and sponsors and their representatives, successors, and assigns, for any and all injuries suffered by me in said event."
- The trial court held the release valid unless there was fraud, misrepresentation, duress, illiteracy, or no chance to read it. It said such releases were barred by public policy only in (1) a common carrier's contract of carriage, (2) a public utility's telephone-service contract, or (3) a condition of employment.

## Holding

- The Court reversed: "The case law in this Commonwealth over the past one hundred years has not altered the holding in Johnson."
- Quoting *Johnson's Adm'x v. Richmond & Danville R.R. Co.*, 86 Va. 975, 978, 11 S.E. 829 (1890): "[T]o hold that it was competent for one party to put the other parties to the contract at the mercy of its own misconduct... can never be lawfully done where an enlightened system of jurisprudence prevails. Public policy forbids it, and contracts against public policy are void."
- *Johnson* was "not based on the fact that the railroad company was a common carrier." Releases from personal-injury liability for future negligence are prohibited "universally."
- Conclusion: "the pre-injury release provision signed by Hiett is prohibited by public policy and, thus, it is void."
- Disposition: affirmed in part (the co-organizer Novins was correctly dismissed because she had no ownership or control of the lake and so no duty to warn; *Busch v. Gaglio*, 207 Va. 343 (1966)), reversed in part, and remanded for retrial.

## What Virginia still allows (per the opinion)

- **Property-damage releases** have been upheld: *C. & O. Ry. Co. v. Telephone Co.*, 216 Va. 858 (1976), where a release for damage to cables raised no public-policy issue; and *Nido v. Ocean Owners' Council*, 237 Va. 664 (1989), a condo bylaw waiver. Footnote 1: the *Nido* bylaw also covered personal injury, but only the property part was at issue.
- **Indemnification against third-party property-damage claims** has been upheld between commercial parties: *Richardson-Wayland v. VEPCO*, 219 Va. 198 (1978). The Court stressed that this was a contract with a private company, "not between VEPCO and a consumer." *Appalachian Power Co. v. Sanders*, 232 Va. 189 (1986), and *Kitchin v. Gary Steel Corp.*, 196 Va. 259 (1954), did not decide whether personal-injury indemnity is valid.
- In none of these cases did the Court decide whether an indemnification clause is valid against a personal-injury claim. That question was left open in 1992.

## Implications for the business-plan options [inferred]

- **Renting a slaughter or cooler trailer to farmers:** a renter waiver cannot release the owner from the owner's own negligence for bodily injury to the renter, for example a defective hoist or a brake failure. A release of claims for damage to the renter's *property* (carcasses, vehicle) is likely enforceable under *C&O*/*Nido*.
- **Hands-on workshops (knives, firearms, live animals):** participant waivers will not stop a negligence claim in Virginia courts. Commercial general liability (CGL) or special-event coverage is the real protection. Waivers can still help prove that risks were disclosed (assumption-of-risk and contributory-negligence defenses, which Virginia retains) [inferred; not stated in the opinion].
- **Indemnity from renters** is a separate doctrine from releases. Later Virginia cases (*Estes Express Lines v. Chopper Express*, 273 Va. 358 (2007); *W.R. Hall v. HRSD*, 273 Va. 350 (2007)) deal with indemnity enforceability between commercial parties. They were not fetched here and need to be checked before relying on renter indemnity.
- Statutory carve-outs, such as Virginia's agritourism inherent-risk statute and equine-activity statute, are separate from *Hiett* and were not fetched in this source.
